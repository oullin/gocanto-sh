Trust in an engineering team is built from small predictions that keep proving true. A
person says a task is blocked and explains why. A manager changes a commitment when the
evidence changes. An incident review describes what happened without first deciding who
should carry the blame.

Honesty matters because every delivery process depends on information arriving early enough
to change a decision.

## Make uncertainty visible

A plan is less useful when its dates are precise but its assumptions are hidden. Teams should
be able to say that an integration is not understood, test coverage is weak, or a dependency
has no safe rollback without being treated as obstructive.

That candour improves planning. It lets the team reduce scope, run a spike, add a guard, or
move the date while those options still exist. Silence converts the same facts into a late
surprise.

Leaders set the conditions. If the response to bad news is punishment or public theatre,
people learn to delay it. If an early warning leads to a useful decision, they learn that
accuracy is valued more than optimism.

## Own mistakes without simplifying them

Accountability does not require finding one person to blame. It requires an accurate account
of the decision, the information available at the time, the controls that failed, and the
change that will reduce recurrence.

Saying “I made the wrong call” is useful when it is true. So is saying that a process made
the wrong call likely: an unsafe default, unclear ownership, a missing test, or an alert that
could not distinguish normal load from an incident.

Public learning only works when follow-up is visible. A retrospective that produces actions
nobody owns teaches the team that candour creates meetings rather than change.

## Delegate decisions with their boundaries

Trusting engineers is not abandoning review. It means being clear about the outcome, risk
budget, and decisions the team owns, then allowing them to choose the implementation.

Healthy review asks for evidence and tests assumptions. Micromanagement repeats the work
while leaving ownership ambiguous. The distinction is whether review improves the decision
or merely proves that the reviewer remains in control.

This is also why I prefer describing work as a professional team rather than a family. The
expectations can be explicit, performance can be discussed honestly, and boundaries do not
need to be disguised as loyalty. I explore that distinction in
[Work is not a family, and that is a good thing](/work-is-not-family-and-that-is-a-good-thing).

Trust is not a statement in a values document. It is what accumulates when people can report
reality and see the organization respond to it.
