Engineering encourages continuous improvement. We refactor code, review incidents, plan the
next quarter, and measure whether a system is getting better. It is easy to apply the same
model to a person until every day becomes preparation for a future version of oneself.

An [Alan Watts talk](https://youtu.be/LE4J93zk40A) made me question that transfer. This is my
response to the talk, not a transcript or an attempt to turn it into management doctrine.

## A role is useful, but it is still a role

“Engineering leader” comes with an interface: make decisions, explain trade-offs, mentor
people, and take responsibility when the plan fails. The role helps a team know what to
expect. Trouble starts when protecting the identity becomes more important than doing the
work.

That can look like defending a decision after the evidence changes, pretending to have an
answer, or treating disagreement as a challenge to status. Remembering that the role is not
the whole person makes it easier to say “I was wrong” without experiencing the correction as
a collapse.

## Deadlines are coordinates, not a place to live

Roadmaps and two-week iterations are useful coordination devices. They are poor measures of
whether the day in front of us has value.

The work still happens in the present: reading the unfamiliar code, listening to the person
who found a risk, or staying with a difficult problem long enough to understand it. A team
that talks only about the next milestone can miss the quality of the process producing it.

Past incidents create a similar trap when they become permanent identities. A failed release
should change controls and judgment. It should not become proof that the team is incapable of
shipping.

## Improvement without permanent inadequacy

There is a difference between learning and treating oneself as a defect backlog. The first
opens possibilities. The second makes completion impossible because each improvement only
reveals another reason not to be satisfied.

For leadership, this changes the question from “How do I fix this person?” to “What is
preventing this person from doing the work they are already capable of doing?” Sometimes the
answer is missing skill, and training helps. Sometimes it is unclear authority, fear, or a
system that rewards the wrong behaviour.

Projects have finish lines; a working life does not. I still plan, review, and improve. The
talk was a reminder not to postpone participation until some imagined final version of
myself arrives.
